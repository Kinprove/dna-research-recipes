# Jewish genealogy: conditional naming, town and DNA clues

> Version 1.0 · 2026-10-07

## Goal

Apply a working hypothesis to Jewish naming, locality and DNA clues without letting one supported proposition establish the others. A proposed father can select a search town; establishing that father still does not establish the child's birthplace, the intended namesake or the ancestor behind every shared segment.

Use the general premise register, hypothesis-testing and dependency-review procedure in [Evidence and proof judgment](evidence-proof-judgment.md). This companion applies it to Jewish clues; it introduces no competing status system. For historical identity mechanisms and their falsifiers, use [Russian-Empire Jewish identity attribution](russian-empire-jewish-identity-attribution.md); for queries and household reconstruction, use [JewishGen search and family reconstruction](jewishgen-search-family-reconstruction.md).

## The failure to prevent

An assumed father acquires a town. The town acquires a migration story. A DNA match gets assigned to that father's branch. The branch label then returns as apparent support for the original father. All four statements can originate in one unproved connection.

Break that loop at the connection. Keep the documented descendant family intact, place the candidate ancestor alongside it, and give the unresolved edge an ID. Every claim reached through that edge must carry the ID until its own support no longer depends on the assumption. [S1–S4]

## 1. Make the Jewish context an input, not a universal rule

Before using a name or town as a prediction, record the community, period, language and record type that make the prediction meaningful. “Jewish” alone is insufficient.

| Clue | Productive conditional use | Inference it does not license |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A child shares a supposed grandfather's given name | In a family following deceased-relative naming, search for the grandfather's death and for other namesakes; check the actual family custom | A death-before-birth deadline or a unique grandfather identity |
| A Hebrew patronymic and an American civil name differ | Keep both recorded forms and look for a document that connects them to the same person | A deterministic translation that makes the candidate fathers identical |
| A family is associated with one town | Search the relevant jurisdiction and record series; preserve whether the town is birthplace, residence or legal registration | A birthplace or migration route for descendants reached through an unproved parent |
| An endogamous match shares substantial DNA | Use a separately documented branch as a research lead and compare rival ancestral paths | A named recent ancestor, a unique path or a relationship proved by the total alone |

Ashkenazi deceased-relative naming is a custom with exceptions; Sephardi families can name children after living relatives. Determine the family's practice before asking the naming question. Americanized given names have no fixed reverse translation. Endogamy can make relationship predictions appear closer and permits more than one ancestral contribution. [S6, S7, S9, S10]

## 2. Separate the propositions before combining clues

Put these distinct propositions in the general register when the case uses them. Preserve the source's actual statement beside the interpretation. Give each rival candidate its own kinship or identity proposition; do not collapse everything into “the Jewish branch is probably right.”

| Premise | Specific question | What settling it leaves open |
| --- | --- | --- |
| K1 — kinship | Is documented D the child of candidate P? | Name equivalence, intended namesake, D's birthplace and individual segment origins |
| A1 — alias | Do these Hebrew, Yiddish and civil name forms identify the same actual person? | Whether that person is D's parent |
| N1 — namesake | Was this child named for this particular person? | Kinship and whether the namesake was deceased |
| C1 — custom | Did this family follow the proposed deceased- or living-relative naming practice at that birth? | Who supplied the name and the actual death date |
| T1 — place | What place role, person and date does this record establish? | Place claims for another person or period, and migration beyond what the source states |
| D1 — DNA attribution | Does this segment come through the proposed ancestor rather than another shared path? | Attribution of other segments or every same-surname match |

K1 and A1 need not rise or fall together. In the Kiev case, a proposed brother connection meets different recorded fathers' names; a compound-name reconciliation is an additional identity hypothesis, not the solution to the relationship question. Test the name reconciliation rather than spending it as established parentage. [S2, S10, S11]

Likewise, changing C1 from a deceased-relative expectation to a living-relative practice removes that route to a death-before-birth inference; it does not disprove K1. A naming clue can direct a death search while N1 and C1 remain unproved. Record the resulting search window as a scenario, not a death fact. [S6, S7, S11]

## 3. Apply the register to Jewish research outputs

| Output | What remains a fact | What depends on K1, the proposed kinship |
| --- | --- | --- |
| An ancestral-town search | The candidate P is recorded in town T, in the role and date the record states | “D's ancestry includes this person associated with T”; D's birthplace needs its own evidence |
| A reconstructed branch | The independently documented parent-child links within D's descendants and within P's family | The bridge between the two families |
| A potential Y-DNA tester | The independently documented descent of that person along the proposed testing line | The prediction that the two lines share the particular candidate ancestor |
| A chromosome-map label | The observed match and segment data | The ancestral attribution assigned through K1 |
| A written family narrative | Literal observations with their source citations | The connecting biography or migration story inferred under K1 |

A candidate's registration or a death during a visit supplies a search place; it does not establish residence, birthplace or itinerary for everyone attached to the hypothetical branch. Keep those place propositions separate even after kinship is established. [S3, S5, S8]

Do not use a hypothesis-built branch, a chromosome-map label or a copied tree as independent confirmation of the assumption that produced it. Track the originating record or observation, not the number of websites displaying it. [S4, S5]

Use the general dependency-review rule when a premise changes. The Jewish-specific check is which of K1, A1, N1, C1, T1 and D1 actually changed: a new naming practice, a town-role correction and a rejected father have different consequences. Keep independently supported descendants and literal records. [S1–S5, S11]

## Worked example: a naming clue becomes a search, not a death date

This is a constructed example, not an asserted historical family.

Rivka's son's birth record names him Leib. A possible father of Rivka is also named Leib. H1 proposes that this man is her father; H2 proposes another same-name man. Rivka's documented spouse and children stay attached to her independently of either candidate father.

First establish which naming practice is evidenced for this family. Even if deceased-relative naming fits, “the child was named Leib” does not show which relative supplied the name or when either candidate died. Keep “search for candidate deaths before the child's birth” as a task; leave both death dates unknown. A living-relative naming practice makes the proposed death deadline inapplicable. [S6, S7]

Under H1, the first candidate's town is a useful place to search for Rivka's marriage or household. Preserve T1's actual record role. In Polish Books of Residents before 1932, permanent legal residence could differ from actual residence; do not carry that collection's rule over to every Jewish record. A record establishing the candidate's registration does not establish Rivka's birthplace, even if H1 is later supported. [S8]

Find a record that distinguishes the fathers through the household, spouse, age, occupation or another independently established identifier. A matching given name alone leaves H1 and H2 alive. If H1 fails, withdraw the ancestral attribution through that candidate; retain Rivka's documented marriage and children, and the candidate's actual town record. If only the supposed naming practice changes, revise the death-search scenario and leave the parentage question open.

## Worked example: an endogamous match cannot close its own bridge

Two separately documented Jewish descendant families share a surname. H1 connects their earliest men as brothers; H2 connects them more distantly; H3 leaves the link unresolved. Use these possibilities to find earlier records and identify appropriate descendants for a proposed comparison. The Nunes Vaz research illustrates the productive distinction between separately traced branches and their unresolved connecting ancestors. [S3]

Keep a DNA match as an observation. If you label it “from H1's father” using the hypothetical bridge, that label is an instance of D1, not a second piece of support for H1. An endogamous cousin can share DNA through additional paths; a documented relationship does not automatically assign every shared segment to that relationship. [S4, S9]

If a proposed DNA test predicts the same result for H1 and H2, it does not distinguish them. A compatible Y-DNA result does not by itself identify a named father or reconstruct a migration itinerary. Do not turn the case presenter's confidence into your conclusion. [S2, S3]

## Prompt craft

```text
Build a conditional research plan for this Jewish ancestral connection.
Use only the supplied records and DNA observations as facts.
Use the general working-hypothesis register. Separate kinship, actual-person
alias, intended namesake, family naming custom, person/date/place role, and
per-segment ancestral attribution. Return the independent starting facts,
live rivals, evidenced community context, next discriminating action and
expected outcomes, record coverage, and dependent outputs for each premise.
Explain what changes if one premise fails and which others remain open.
Keep documented descendants intact. Do not translate civil names into a
unique Hebrew name, infer a death date from a naming custom, count copied
records as independent support, or use a hypothesis-derived DNA label to
confirm its own ancestral bridge. If rivals predict the same observation,
say that it does not distinguish them. Give no invented confidence numbers.
```

Before reusing an earlier answer, supply the current premise register and revision dates. Ask specifically whether a solved kinship has been allowed to verify an unsolved alias, naming, place or segment proposition.
