# Recipe: Name mismatch in a Russian-Empire Jewish family — which branch, and what DNA can still say

## Goal

When a Jewish person from the Pale, Lithuania, Belarus, Ukraine, Latvia or Congress Poland turns up under a given name, patronymic or surname that fits neither candidate branch — or fits the wrong one — decide which branch (if either) they belong to: which record features and household-structure signals actually discriminate, in what order to check them, which naming and re-registration mechanisms must be carried as explicit competing hypotheses, and what DNA can still contribute when the tree carries endogamy plus repeated pedigree collapse. The statutory dates cited below are the Russian imperial regime of the Pale guberniyas; Congress Poland kept its own civil-status registration and surname chronology, and Courland and Bessarabia have their own — confirm the jurisdiction before applying a date.

## The mental model (a name is a household label; the register is a ledger of registration)

Three inversions of the model's defaults do all the work here.

1. **Names in this system are written *after a person's position in a household*, not after their biology.** Surnames were imposed late and unevenly (the 1804 statute, re-imposed in the 1830s, applied community by community, clerk by clerk). 1850 revisions carry brothers under different surnames in one household (Факторович / Буряковский; Хабенский / Резник) or in two families of one list (Кикинзун / Говхман, same patronymic); one man carries two ("он же"); treat one surname across an early post-imposition household as a clerk's label, not a paternity statement. A patronymic is normally built from the first element of a double name — the mask rule in **jewish-name-variant-search** — but records also use the second element or reverse the order (the Chernyakhov entries write "daughter of Yosef" and "of Yosef-Itskhak" for a father called Itskhak-Yosef), so add those forms to the masks as explicit exceptions; and the patronymic is taken from the *household head* — a grandfather, stepfather, guardian, even the husband — when the father was dead, absent, or unknown to the scribe. So a name mismatch is a **household-structure event to be explained**, not evidence against kinship; a name match is not evidence for it. (J-Roots 3733, 4667, 1107; GeneaVlogger Tulchyn; Velkovich)
2. **Revision lists, family lists and recruit lists are ledgers of *registration* (приписка), updated from the previous ledger.** They record who is *ascribed* to a taxpaying society and what happened to each entry since last time (died, given as recruit, absent, transferred), not who was born, not who lived where. "Проживать" and "быть приписанным" are, in the archivists' phrase, "три большие разницы." Absence from a list has documented non-death explanations — exemption, registration elsewhere, an additional list, transfer, service, conversion, concealment — and each has to be excluded before absence means anything. (J-Roots 36, 1272; Древо Жизни 3802; Уральская 1206)
3. **Under endogamy plus pedigree collapse, autosomal DNA separates two branches of one family only where the fully specified pedigrees make the predictions differ for a tester you actually have.** Adjacent-degree distributions overlap; recent collapse across the two branches can make A's descendants B's descendants too; the population layer adds sharing no recent tree explains. So never report a placement from total cM against "branch A" versus "branch B" descendants. Write the prediction per tester under each hypothesis first. A difference of one expected *degree* inside overlapping total-cM distributions is weak likelihood evidence, usable only through a pedigree-aware model; a *qualitative* difference — recent IBD under one hypothesis and population background under the other, or a resolved uniparental mismatch — can decide. (J-Roots 3733; GeneaVlogger Miglin; Estes 2022; Family Locket Hopper case)

## Hard rules — apply before you place anyone

1. **Parse the name string before you judge it.** Decide, per token, whether it is a surname, a given name, the first or second element of a double name, a patronymic, or a matronymic-derived surname — and whether a "different" name is the same name in Yiddish/Hebrew/Russian dress. A mis-parse manufactures a phantom mismatch.
2. **A name mismatch never changes the branch verdict on its own; the household chain does.** Chain the person through household number → previous household number → age at previous revision → "выбыло" column → additional lists before you interpret any name.
3. **Find the приписка first, then the lists of *that* society.** A person absent from the lists of the town they lived in is not evidence of anything until you know where they were ascribed.
4. **A relation label in a revision list is written relative to the head of that entry, so it changes when the head changes** (elder brother → "brother of the head" when the younger inherited headship; a man who is both nephew and son-in-law can be written as either). Never read "сын" as necessarily biological, "племянник" in a pre-1850 list as brother's son, or a fostered child's new surname as adoption of record.
5. **Carry every plausible re-registration mechanism as a live hypothesis with its own falsifying record feature** (table below). Dismissing one because it sounds dramatic, or picking one because it sounds dramatic, are the same error.
6. **Base rate before drama.** Scribal and double-name variance and head-of-household patronymics are the explanations to exhaust first; recruit-levy engineering is real but requires a levy year, an age-eligible boy and a motive — demand all three before invoking it.
7. **Ages are estimates.** Two to three years of drift between lists is ordinary. A larger discrepancy is a question about the field, not an identity verdict: check the original entry and what date its age column is reckoned from (the last revision, a decree, "по наружному виду"). The 1874–75 male lists carry both a documentary and a by-appearance age column — introduced, in the archivist's account, because ages by different papers disagreed, with "no system" in whether the two columns agree — so read them against each other before treating the person as somebody else or the age as falsified.
8. **Absence from a list is a *registration* fact.** Before it means death, rule out: tax-exemption, registration to another society, entry in an additional list, transfer ("перечислен") to another household/estate, conversion into a Christian society under a new name, military service, and concealment.
9. **DNA goes last, and only after you have written, per mechanism, which tester's result would differ.** A raw total against "branch A descendants" vs "branch B descendants" is not a verdict under endogamy plus collapse. If no available tester's prediction differs, the verdict is "undecidable by DNA with current testers," and you name the tester who would decide it.
10. **The output is a hypothesis ledger, and its status column is the placement.** Mechanism, evidence for, evidence against, the record that would settle it, the DNA observation that would settle it, current status — the placement is the one mechanism left standing once the others' falsifiers have been found, and "open" while none has been.

## What the HUMAN must own

- **Which link is actually in doubt.** Father, mother, both, or the person's identity itself — and, given the dates, which mechanisms are even *possible* (a recruit substitution needs a levy in range; a stepchild needs a prior marriage or widowhood). The model will happily weigh mechanisms the chronology already excludes.
- **The registration geography.** Deciding which мещанское/купеческое society the family was ascribed to — from a later document that states it (a metrical entry's estate phrase "Сорокский мещанин," the 1897 census приписка column, a passport or причисление file) — is a research decision the model cannot make from a place of residence. The сословие marker and место приписки are hard identity discriminators, and a repeated name down a pedigree is a namesake before it is a match — the gates in **kinship-claim-verification**.
- **The mechanism verdict.** Which competing re-registration story survives the record features. The model ranks stories by narrative fit; you rank them by which falsifier you actually found.
- **The "differs / does not differ" call for every DNA tester** and the decision to stop asking DNA when nothing differs.
- **The sufficiency ruling on an absence** — whether the list *should* have contained the person (registered there, taxable, not in service, not converted) before absence carries weight.

## Where AI reliably goes wrong (the named failure modes)

- **Name-polarity error.** Mismatch → "not kin"; match → "kin." Reality: brothers under different surnames in one 1850 household (Факторович / Буряковский in a Radomysl-district list; Хабенский / Резник) or across two families of one list (Кикинзун / Говхман, same patronymic); a son-in-law who took a wife's surname; two brothers who re-registered into another society and took a wife's and a brother's surname — "to dodge the army," in the forum's reading; a grandson registered with the *maternal* grandfather's surname and patronymic while his brothers kept the father's; a wife's patronymic silently replaced by her husband's given name for the rest of her life. And the converse: a patronymic-derived surname (Aaronov) or a simple occupational one (Waxer) adopted by several unrelated families in a town of thousands. (J-Roots 4667, 3733; GeneaVlogger Miglin, Tulchyn)
- **Token mis-parse.** Reads a mother's patronymic as her maiden name (Jewish entries usually give the mother as name + patronymic, no maiden name); reads the second element of a double name as a patronymic ("Шлема Ицка" is one man, not Shlema son of Itsko — which turned a "contradictory" nephew into an ordinary one); cannot tell whether an unaccompanied "Шомшонович" or "Мориц Пейсах Мошкавич" is surname / given name / patronymic; merges Гершон with Герш (different names) or splits Люба from Либа (one woman). (J-Roots 4667, 1272, 5084; GeneaVlogger Q&A, Tolchin live)
- **Vital-record fallacy.** Treats a revision or family list as a residence census and a birth register: absence = death or non-existence; presence = residence; listed age = birth year; missing women = dead or unmarried (early revisions and specific men-only lists — an 1874 "male part of the family" list, an 1875 town list — omit them by design; the standard 1874 family-list form has a female section, so know which form you hold; and girls' births often went unregistered in the first place — see **jewishgen-research**). (J-Roots 4667, 1202; Древо Жизни 3802; GeneaVlogger Tolchin live)
- **Ignoring the cross-reference machinery.** Never asks for the previous household number, the age-at-last-revision column, the "выбыло" reason, the additional lists, or the levy number — the columns that carry identity when names do not. (GeneaVlogger revision-list streams; J-Roots 4667)
- **Label literalism.** Takes "сын" as biological, "усыновление" as a recorded act, "приёмыш/воспитанник" as formal adoption or as a stepchild, and a fostered child's new surname as permanent — when the same person appears under birth and adoptive names in different entries of the same year. Check the marriage entry: in the attested case it was the only record carrying the birth surname and true patronymic. (Уральская 146; J-Roots 4667, 1107)
- **Drama over base rate.** Explains a single anomalous line with cantonist substitution when a scribe's line-slip, a double name, or the household head's name is the base-rate cause — or, symmetrically, dismisses levy engineering in a family with an eligible boy in a levy year. (J-Roots 4667; Уральская 1206)
- **Autosomal branch-picking under collapse.** Reads "more cM with branch A's descendants" as "belongs to A"; adds path averages; reports a confident placement with a number attached; never asks whether any observation predicts differently under the two hypotheses. (see the DNA section; mechanics routed to the sibling recipes)
- **Y/surname mismatch = NPE.** Under a documented surname split within one patriline, a Y match across two surnames is the *expected* outcome, not a contradiction. (routed to **ydna-mtdna-interpretation**)
- **Anglo transfer.** "No death record → still alive," "census = where they lived," "surname = family" — each false for this system (Odessa Maidenburgs were registered to Soroki). "The marriage will be in the bride's community" is the usual case here too (see **jewishgen-research**) but not a rule: a couple registered to Shklov and Mogilev married in Smolensk, so absence from both home books is not evidence. (J-Roots 1107, 1272)

## Techniques that work

### 1. The order of checks (cheapest and most decisive first)

1. **Freeze the doubtful link and the chronology window** — which levies, deaths, marriages and remarriages fall inside it.
2. **Parse the name.** Surname / given / double-name elements / patronymic (masculine and feminine forms) / matronymic-derived surname (Дворкин, Эстеркин run *in parallel* with patronymic ones). List the Yiddish, Hebrew and Russian forms of each given name and expect a different form in each record type; build the search masks with **jewish-name-variant-search**, adding the second-element and reversed double-name forms this recipe attests. (Velkovich; GeneaVlogger Q&A)
3. **Establish the приписка, then work in *that* society's lists.** Start from the latest document that states the estate and society and only then go back into the nineteenth century. Family lists (посемейные списки) were compiled only for families ascribed to that society; a Mogilev list even had a separate section for "Jews living outside Mogilev province, ascribed to the Mogilev town society." (J-Roots 36)
4. **Chain the household across lists.** Household number this list ↔ former household number ↔ page; age at this revision over age at last revision; the "по последней ревизии состояло и после оной прибыло" and "из того числа выбыло" columns (умер / отдан в рекруты / в отлучке / перечислен); the additional lists (дополнительные ревизские сказки) for anyone missed in a main revision or transferred in from elsewhere — they continue after the last full revision of 1858 (additional lists of 1864, 1873 and 1874–1890 are cited in the threads); then the 1874–75 male lists with *two* age columns ("по ревизским сказкам и указам Казённой палаты о причислении и перечислении до 1871 г." and "по наружному виду"); then the family lists. A person sitting at the expected former number with a consistent last-revision age *is* the person; the name is a variant. That is how Meer, son of Elia — household 671 in an 1875 list — was confirmed as family 214 of 1858 on page 440. (GeneaVlogger revision-list streams; J-Roots 4667, 36)
5. **Read relation labels against the current head, and watch them drift.** Treat "племянник" in a pre-1850 list as "relative," not "brother's son," until the household chain says otherwise; by 1858 the modern meaning is usual. "При семье брата," "зять," "воспитанник," "приёмный," "приёмыш," "пасынок" describe the *household*, not descent. A man written "nephew" in 1850 as the head's brother's son, whose wife that year carries the head's name as her patronymic, is son-in-law as well as nephew — inside the head's household that patronymic makes her his daughter unless another man of that name can be shown as her father; when he reappears in 1858 as "зять" with a wife whose patronymic excludes her being the head's daughter, the label has outlived the marriage that created it. A younger brother listed as head with an older "brother of the head" means the father died and headship passed. (J-Roots 4667)
6. **Test age, sequence and naming fit.** Two to three years of age drift is nothing; a larger gap sends you to the original entry and its date basis, not to a second person. A boy who appears in a household with no birth entry there and an age off the family's spacing raises the re-registration hypothesis — it does not prove it: births were registered late, elsewhere or not at all, so require a recruit, quittance, причисление/перечисление or equivalent record before concluding he was registered rather than born into the household. Ashkenazi naming after the recently dead is a signed clue: a child named for a man who died two years earlier points at the branch that man belonged to (the Miglin Abram born 1828 dated his grandfather's death to about 1827; the Chernyakhov Itsko-Yos born 1883 honoured the mother's father who died in 1880). (J-Roots 1202, 1107, 3733; GeneaVlogger Miglin)
7. **Read the metrical books for what they encode.** In the Pale guberniyas Jewish metrical books were mandated in 1835 and kept from 1857 by the казённый раввин, a community-elected, government-confirmed official (Congress Poland's акты гражданского состояния follow a different regime). The parents' estate phrase gives the registration society; the mother is name + patronymic; a remarriage entry states девица / разводная / вдова and the widow's surname may be her first husband's or her maiden one (or both coincide when she married a same-surname cousin); a marriage may have been registered in a third town, so its absence from both home communities is nothing. Look for later marginalia — some, not all, adoptions were later noted in the register; the post-1891 court procedure inscribed the adoption on the family-held birth *certificate*, so the archived register may carry nothing; a Soviet-era slip addressed to the Klintsy ZAGS office was glued onto a 1905 birth entry to record a 1917 adoption with a new surname *and* new given name and patronymic. (J-Roots 3733, 4667, 1107)
8. **Pull the recruit and conscription files.** Personal recruit files (личные дела по рекрутскому призыву), recruit family lists (рекрутские посемейные списки — where "приёмыш" appears), draft lists and causes "о причислении к призывному участку." The levy is a documented motive for deliberate re-registration, and the exemption claim is where a substitution is written down — but a name in a recruit list may also be a purchased substitute, not kin at all (see **russian-empire-immigration-research**). "По 95 набору отдан в рекруты" dates a surrender to 1830 by levy number. (J-Roots 36, 4667, 1107)
9. **Only then DNA**, with the prediction table below.

### 2. The mechanisms to model as competing hypotheses

| Mechanism | Biology vs. registration | Record feature that confirms it (a label alone only raises the hypothesis) | DNA observation that differs |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Variant form** — double-name element, Yiddish/Hebrew/Russified form, feminine patronymic, index error | unchanged | intact household chain; same last-revision age; a death or tax entry using the other element of the double name (Хаим = Хайкель) | none — never test |
| **Head-of-household patronymic** — grandfather, guardian, stepfather or husband written where the father belongs, typically after the father's death or absence | unchanged | the father's "умер / отдан в рекруты / в отлучке" precedes the drift; older siblings carry the real patronymic; the name written is the head's | none if the head is the paternal grandfather; if the written name is the *maternal* grandfather, a resolved Y mismatch with that grandfather's patriline excludes "he really was that man's late son"; a Y match supports it without proving it |
| **Heir naming** — a grandson registered with a son-less grandfather's surname and patronymic | unchanged | one sibling differs, the rest do not; the grandfather has no sons | a resolved Y mismatch with the grandfather's patriline excludes "son of" and leaves "named for"; a match supports the grandfather's patriline — never the man — and excludes the documented father only if his patriline is independently resolved and different (§3); when the two patrilines are kin (a documentary check), Y separates nothing |
| **Stepchild** (пасынок) — wife's child from a prior marriage under the stepfather's names | father differs, mother same | the mother's earlier marriage or her remarriage entry as вдова/разводная, and the child's birth entry under the first husband; "приёмыш" in a recruit family list only raises the hypothesis — the forum's reading of one such entry was "most likely a stepson, but possibly an orphan taken in by relatives, or something else" | a resolved Y mismatch with the stepfather's patriline excludes "his son"; full-vs-half against the stepfather's children (tested siblings only, see §3); mt unchanged |
| **Fostered child** (воспитанник/приёмный) — child placed with relatives or an unrelated household, for reasons including a parent's death; entered under the foster household's surname with the foster father's name as patronymic, or under his own surname with the label alone (both forms are attested) | both parents differ from the registered ones | a parent's death entry near the birth; birth entry under the original parents; "приёмный" once in a revision; marriage entry reverting to birth surname and true patronymic | depends on *who* the caregivers were: father's brother → Y same, mt differs; mother's sister → Y differs, mt same; unrelated → both differ. Identify the caregivers and the original parents first, or the test is uninterpretable; a mismatch excludes, a match only supports |
| **Levy engineering** — boy hidden, age lowered, marked "в отлучке," registered into another household (as its exempt only son, or under its surname), re-registered into another society under a wife's or brother's surname, names altered at surrender | unchanged | levy year + eligible age; no birth entry in the registered household; age off the family's spacing; recruit file / quittance; later "перечислен" back; a re-registration file into another society | a resolved Y mismatch with the registered patriline excludes biological sonship to that household (then test the proposed biological patriline); recent IBD at close range with the proposed biological family against background-only sharing with the registered one supports the re-registration — the two households' own kinship and any other path between them set the background the difference must exceed (§3); when the households are patrilineal kin, Y separates nothing |
| **Son-in-law took the wife's surname** — no sons, or a prestigious rabbinical line | children are the surname family's grandchildren *through the mother* | his marriage entry with the birth surname; his brothers under it; "зять" in the wife's father's household | a resolved Y mismatch excludes patrilineal membership in the surname family; marriage and household records, not DNA, establish the surname-taking; autosomal predictions follow from the two explicit pedigrees (his own siblings' descendants are unrelated to the surname line) |
| **Surname split / double surname** at imposition — brothers fixed under different surnames, or one man "он же" | unchanged | same patronymic, same or adjacent household; "он же" in a marriage entry | none; a cross-surname Y match is expected |
| **Conversion** — new given name, godparent's surname, re-registration into a Christian society | unchanged | parish book "принявший православие из евреев"; a причисление file to a Christian мещанское society "под именем Павла" | none |
| **Second unrelated family** with the same (patronymic or occupational) surname in the same town | different people | separate household chains; different приписка and birthplace ("приписка и место рождения — разные; вряд ли родственники") | a resolved Y mismatch separates them; a Y match at low resolution does not join them — unrelated Ashkenazi families share Y lineages; autosomal is population background only |

### 3. What DNA can still contribute — and the test that has to be passed first

- **Write the prediction table before touching a match list.** Under H1 ("child of brother A") vs H2 ("child of brother B"), a descendant of A's other children sits one full cousin degree closer under H1 (second cousin rather than third, say) — a real gap in expectation that still lies inside the overlap of the two distributions at second-cousin range and beyond; Ashkenazi endogamy adds population-level identity on top — and that layer is dominated by founder-era collapse (Leo Cooper's point, relayed by GeneaVlogger), so it cannot be subtracted by knowing your recent tree; and *recent* collapse (cousins marrying cousins across A and B) makes A's descendants also B's descendants, so H1 and H2 can predict the **same** sharing for them. Net: a raw total against either branch's descendants is not a discriminator. Where the specified pedigrees do predict different amounts — the Hopper case computed about 273 versus 164 cM for two candidate fathers and still declined to decide on one 239 cM observation — the difference is weak likelihood evidence for a pedigree-aware model and a reason to test more descendants of the candidate brothers, not a placement. The additive and WATO mechanics that fail here, and the pedigree-aware comparison that can carry weak evidence, are documented in **ai-for-dna-research** and **unknown-parentage-wato**; do not re-derive them. (GeneaVlogger IAJGS recap; Estes 2022; Family Locket Gephi; Family Locket Hopper case)
- **Establish the collapse you are modelling, and know what the tools cannot see.** GEDmatch's runs-of-homozygosity check only sees collapse on *both* parental sides; one-sided collapse (one parent from the tight community) is invisible to it, so a clean result does not clear the tree. Two or three collapses are not endogamy; an Ashkenazi tree carries the population layer and may also carry recent collapse — enumerate and model the recent collapses, and treat the population layer as a floor you cannot model, only bound. Matches assigned to both parental sides are an endogamy indicator when both parents are linked and both sides share the population (about 11–14 % of matches in Estes's endogamous examples, under 1 % in her non-endogamous one — case observations, not a threshold), not a sign the placement is wrong. Thresholds, pile-up regions and the genetic-pioneer tactic are **jewish-dna-research**'s. (Family Locket Hopper case; Family Locket Gephi; Estes 2022)
- **Ask the questions that stay answerable:**
  - **Patriline (Y-DNA)** — informative only when the competing mechanisms *differ in the father's patriline* (stepchild, fostering by the mother's kin or strangers, son-in-law surname, substitution into an unrelated household, a second unrelated family, heir naming after a maternal grandfather) and useless when they share it (a paternal grandfather written as father, surname split, conversion, variants). The logic is directional: a mismatch resolved at a level adequate for the two recent lineages *excludes* the hypothesis that shares the patriline; a match *supports* a shared patriline at some depth and never identifies which man. Brothers A and B share a Y: Y can never choose between them. Which testers to recruit and what a match can establish → **ydna-mtdna-interpretation**.
  - **Matriline (mtDNA)** — informative when the mechanisms differ in the *mother* (fostering by the father's kin; child of a first vs. a second wife; a widow's child), with the same directional logic: a resolved mismatch excludes, a match supports and, in a population with few founding maternal lineages, supports weakly.
  - **Full-vs-half sibling** between the disputed person and a sibling of secure parentage — decisive only when the siblings *themselves* are tested and read by IBD state, and it discriminates by genome-wide proportion, not by density or mere presence: full siblings are fully identical (IBD2) over about a quarter of the genome, ordinary half siblings over essentially none, and endogamy or related mothers add only short, scattered fully-identical patches (Estes 2024 shows them in first cousins with related mothers). When the non-shared parents are themselves brothers or first cousins the proportion is about an eighth or a thirty-second — standard inheritance read off the pedigree; an eighth is still separable from a quarter, a thirty-second sits near the endogamy-scatter floor — so write the proportion each hypothesis predicts before you read the result. Their children compare as first cousins versus half first cousins: the half-cousin expectation is half the full one, the observed ranges overlap, and endogamy lifts the half-cousin side — one comparison decides only when it falls outside the overlap; inside it, or when the non-shared parents are kin, take several comparisons or a pedigree-aware model. The J-Roots maternal-grandfather-surname family did the sibling-level test — the grandfather and his younger brother tested as sharing a father — with the method unreported. (J-Roots 3733)
  - **The out-married line** — a descendant of A's wife's family, when she came from another town (the forum's explanation of "дальний брак": small communities were all kin within three or four generations), predicts recent sharing under H1 and background-only sharing under H2. She was still Ashkenazi, so the difference is recent IBD versus population background: judge it by segment length, not total, at close range where a long single segment is not background (thresholds → **jewish-dna-research**). It is one useful class of tester whose prediction differs; any sufficiently close collateral line exclusive to one hypothesis is another. Choose testers by whether their prediction differs, never by their cM. (J-Roots 1107)
- **If nothing differs, say so.** "Undecidable by DNA with current testers; a patrilineal descendant of B / a matrilineal descendant of the first wife would decide it" is a correct verdict. A confident placement is not.

## Worked mini-examples (the reasoning logic)

- **Four "fathers" for one mother (Chernyakhov, 1868–1883).** Yokheved, wife of Hersh Zimmerman, is entered across six births twice with no patronymic, then as "daughter of Yosef," then "of Yosef-Itskhak," then twice "of Yakov-Moshe." The double name explains the middle two; the last two follow Itskhak-Yosef's death in 1880, and Yakov-Moshe was his father. Read it as head substitution: after the death the clerk wrote her by the surviving head's name — the dead man's orphaned son lived at Yakov-Moshe's — and the thread's copy-error alternative fails because a slip does not reproduce the right grandfather in two different years; a son named Itsko-Yos in 1883 honours the dead man. Every entry is the same woman; a name-mismatch reading would have cut about sixty relatives out of the tree. (J-Roots 3733)
- **Nephew and son-in-law at once (Kolyshki, 1850→1858).** In 1850 the head Ошер's household holds his brother with that brother's son Довид Иоиров — a structural nephew, not a bare label — and Довид's wife Рывка Ошерова, whom the record gives only by patronymic; inside Ошер's household that makes her his daughter unless another Ошер can be shown as her father, so Довид was nephew and son-in-law at once, a cousin-marriage. In 1858 the same man (same name, same birth year) is written "зять" with a different wife, Мера Шаева, born 1838, whose patronymic rules her out as another daughter of Ошер: the first marriage had ended — by death or divorce, the record does not say — and the label was carried forward "по инерции" from his old standing in the household. The label tracks position relative to the head, not descent, and outlives the fact that created it. In the same lists two brothers with the same patronymic carry different surnames (Кикинзун / Говхман), and one is "перечислен в другую семью" to a third brother's household. (J-Roots 4667)
- **The recruit who was not in the additional list (Zhytomyr, 1816–1834).** The 1834 revision shows "Елю-Овсей-Иось," entered "from those added in the 1816 additional list, no. 169" and "given as recruit at the 95th levy" (1830). The additional list at no. 169 holds only "Эль Герш-Вольфович, ½." Additional lists could omit a triple name — but the entry sits oddly close to the word "сын," and, as the forum put it, at surrender they could "chemistry the names to fool the authorities." Correct verdict: two live hypotheses (clerk's abbreviation vs. levy manipulation), each with a record that would settle it — the original page of the 1816 additional list, and the recruit file for the 95th levy of 1830; not a placement. (J-Roots 4667)
- **Three girls "in unknown absence" (1834).** Parents dead in 1828, brothers present, daughters aged 13, 16, 18 marked absent, whereabouts unknown. The entries establish absence, not death and not emigration; the forum's explanations — sent into service as nannies or servants, a family not saying where — are hypotheses to test, not findings. (J-Roots 4667)
- **A "three-year-old" and a man born in 1868 — compatible once the record's date is read.** A researcher rejected a three-year-old Meer Lunts because her Meer was born in 1868 and she took the entry for an 1889 census. The archivist dated the entry to 1868, which makes its three-year-old born about 1865 — inside the ordinary two-to-three-year drift of revision lists — said the entry "may very well be" her man, and sent her to the original in Vilnius. The apparent twenty-year conflict was the researcher's assumed record date, not the record's; the remaining three-year drift is compatible with identity and still needs the original entry and further correlation before it becomes one. (J-Roots 1202)
- **Fostered at two, birth names only at her wedding.** A great-grandmother adopted after her mother's death by a childless family from a neighbouring settlement lived and died under the adoptive father's surname and patronymic and bore her children under them; only her marriage entry gives her birth surname and real father's patronymic. In another family the "приёмный" appeared exactly once, in an 1850 revision, and was the only trace of the birth surname. (Orthodox Ural cases — the record form, not the confession, is what transfers.) (Уральская 146)
- **Registered here, living there.** Jews lived in Kherson and were ascribed to Aleshki (or the reverse); by the second half of the century, when leaving a community and re-registering became harder, the forum calls this "very frequent." Nearly every Maidenburg in Odessa's books was a Soroki мещанин. The search goes to Soroki's lists, and Odessa's silence means nothing. (J-Roots 1272)
- **Absent from two revisions, born there all the same.** Two Yaroslavl families told the 1897 census they were born locally yet are missing from the 8th and 9th revisions. A participant proposed tax exemption — for instance state service — as the likeliest cause; the tenth revision had not yet been checked, so it stays a hypothesis. Either way, a revision was never a population census. (Древо Жизни 3802)
- **Boys who did not exist until they did.** A 1678 census gives a sexton four sons; the 1680 Siberian census of the same man gives seven. The omission is documented; the motive is not — a member's aside that they were hidden "so they would not be taken as recruits" is anachronistic as worded — recruit levies are a later institution — and the motive is unattested here. (Уральская 1206)

## Pitfalls worth internalizing

- It rules on a name before it has chained the household — and the chain would have settled it.
- It looks for the family in the town they lived in, not the society they were ascribed to.
- It reads a fostered child's new names as permanent and never checks the marriage entry.
- It treats women's absence from a men-only list as information.
- It reaches for the cantonist story on one anomalous line, and never checks for a levy year.
- It asks autosomal DNA to choose between brothers, and reports a number.
- It tests a patriline when both hypotheses share it — or reads a Y match as "this man."
